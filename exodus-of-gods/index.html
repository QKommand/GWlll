THE EXODUS OF THE GODS

Volume V // The Divine and Human Age

The Exodus of the Gods

On the final withdrawal from Arah, the end of direct Divine rule, and the beginning of a world left increasingly in mortal hands.

Compiled and Annotated By

Commander Q

Captain Commander of the Vanguard Knights
Researcher of Tyrian History and the Mists

Author's Preface

The second withdrawal

The Gods had withdrawn once before.

During the height of the Divine Age, their radiance made prolonged contact dangerous to mortals. They gradually stopped appearing openly and retreated into Arah, where they continued to govern, preserve artifacts, receive petitions, and influence the world around them.

That was not the Exodus.

The Exodus came only after the Gift of Magic, the division of the Bloodstone, Abaddon's rebellion, and a Divine war severe enough to turn the Crystal Sea into desert.

In 0 AE, the remaining Gods made a different choice. This time they did not withdraw behind the walls of their city.

They left physical Tyria.

Historical Caution // Why They Left

Later Human religious traditions describe the withdrawal as an act of restraint: direct Divine intervention had repeatedly produced consequences that endangered the world the Gods intended to protect. This interpretation is important, but it survives through religious testimony rather than a complete record of the deliberations among the Five.

— Commander Q

I

A pantheon changed by war

Abaddon's defeat left the Divine order fundamentally changed.

The God who had once governed knowledge, secrets, and water alongside the rest of the pantheon was now imprisoned within the Realm of Torment. His worship was suppressed, his name removed from accepted religious life, and the surviving pantheon continued as Five.

The world around them had also changed.

The Crystal Sea had been devastated during the war. The Crystal Desert and Desolation remained as permanent evidence of what Divine conflict could do to the physical world.

The magical crisis that began among mortals had ended with Gods fighting one another.

Commander Q // Observation

The lesson of the war was larger than Abaddon. If Gods could destroy landscapes while settling their own disputes, then victory did not remove the danger of Divine intervention. It proved it.

II

The Five remain in Arah

Abaddon's defeat did not instantly empty Arah.

The remaining Gods still possessed the city that had served as the center of Divine life for generations. Its temples, reliquaries, vaults, palaces, gardens, arsenals, and collections remained around them.

The original Bloodstone was no longer among those treasures. It had been divided and removed from the city as part of the response to the magical wars.

Abaddon's reliquary remained, though later hidden and removed from accepted worship.

Other collections continued to preserve artifacts and knowledge gathered during the Divine Age, including works inherited from civilizations far older than Arah itself.

For a brief final period, the city still housed the Gods who had survived the conflict.

III

The decision to leave Tyria

The remaining Gods eventually decided that they would no longer rule the physical world through direct presence.

Later religious accounts explain this choice through the consequences of Divine intervention. The Gods had attempted to guide mortal civilizations, regulate magic, answer appeals, and reshape the world when they believed intervention necessary.

Those actions had accomplished extraordinary things. They had also produced extraordinary damage.

The Gift of Magic had been followed by destructive wars. Restricting that Gift helped provoke Abaddon's rebellion. The resulting Divine conflict destroyed a sea and scarred entire regions.

Withdrawal offered another path.

Instead of remaining physical rulers who could be drawn repeatedly into the struggles of the world, the Gods would place distance between themselves and Tyria.

Research Note // Motive

The broad decision to withdraw is established. Claims about the individual motives of Dwayna, Balthazar, Melandru, Lyssa, or Grenth should remain separate from the historical narrative unless a surviving source records them directly.

IV

The Exodus

In 0 AE, the remaining Gods departed physical Tyria and withdrew into the Mists.

The event became known as the Exodus of the Gods.

Unlike the earlier retreat recorded through Malchor's statues, this was not simply the end of public appearances.

Arah itself was left behind.

The Gods no longer lived behind its walls, received mortal visitors in the city, or governed Tyria from a physical Divine capital.

The age in which mortals and Gods occupied the same city had ended.

Commander Q // Observation

The first retreat changed how mortals could see their Gods. The Exodus changed where the Gods existed.

V

Beyond the physical world

Departure from Tyria did not mean that the Gods ceased to exist.

They entered the Mists, where Divine realms and domains continued beyond the physical world.

Grenth remained connected with the Underworld and the judgment of the dead. Other Gods likewise continued to possess realms, servants, temples, and spiritual influence beyond Tyria.

Abaddon remained imprisoned within the Realm of Torment rather than joining the Five in their new relationship with the world.

The Exodus therefore created distance rather than disappearance.

Mortals could no longer travel to Arah and expect to find the pantheon physically waiting there, but the Mists remained connected to mortal religion, death, prophecy, and magic.

VI

The Gods were distant, not silent

The Exodus did not immediately sever every connection between the Gods and their followers.

Priests continued to worship them. Temples remained active. Divine blessings could still be sought, and visions, miracles, spiritual encounters, and other signs of Divine power continued to appear throughout later Human history.

The Gods could still act through servants and powers associated with their realms.

What had ended was the ordinary physical arrangement of the Divine Age.

No longer did the pantheon live in a city where mortal rulers could simply cross the world and petition them face to face.

Faith now had to cross a greater distance.

VII

Arah after the Gods

The departure of the Gods did not end the importance of Arah.

The city became the capital of Orr, and the Orrians continued living among the temples, monuments, vaults, gardens, palaces, and sacred places left behind by the pantheon.

Few civilizations inherited anything comparable.

Their capital had once housed the Gods themselves. Ancient artifacts remained within its vaults. Divine structures still shaped its streets. Temples preserved the worship of beings who had once physically occupied the same city.

Arah therefore became both a functioning Human capital and a monument to an age its inhabitants believed might not be permanently over.

Orrian tradition continued to preserve the hope that the Gods might one day return.

Commander Q // Observation

Orr did not inherit the ruins of the Gods. It inherited their home.

VIII

History is divided by the Exodus

The departure of the Gods became important enough that later Human chronology measured history around it.

The year of the Exodus became 0 AE — After the Exodus.

Events before it could be measured backward as BE, while the years that followed counted forward through a world in which the Gods no longer lived physically among their followers.

The calendar makes the scale of the transition clear.

This was not remembered as another royal succession, another war, or another migration.

It became one of the boundaries by which an entire civilization understood time.

IX

Mortal kingdoms inherit their future

Humanity did not lose its Gods, but it lost the form of Divine government that had defined the previous age.

Cantha, Elona, Orr, Ascalon, and the other Human societies that developed across the world increasingly faced their political futures through mortal rulers, institutions, armies, guilds, and religious orders.

The Gods remained objects of devotion and sources of spiritual authority, but kings could no longer rely on a Divine court physically ruling from Arah.

Human civilizations would now rise, divide, conquer, ally, and collapse beneath Gods who were worshiped from afar.

The problems of the world had become increasingly the responsibility of the people living within it.

X

Divine decisions remain in the world

The Gods left Tyria, but the consequences of their actions did not leave with them.

The divided Bloodstones remained scattered through the world, continuing to influence the movement and use of magic.

The ruins created during the war against Abaddon remained.

The Crystal Desert remained.

The Desolation remained.

Arah remained filled with Divine and ancient relics.

Abaddon remained alive within his prison.

Withdrawal prevented new direct rule, but it did not erase the structures, artifacts, enemies, and magical systems created during the Divine Age.

Commander Q // Observation

The Gods removed themselves from Tyria. They could not remove their history from it.

XI

The God who was removed from memory

The accepted pantheon that emerged from the Exodus was not the same pantheon that had once governed from Arah.

Abaddon's name was removed from public worship. His imagery was suppressed, and later Human traditions increasingly remembered him as a fallen enemy rather than one of the Gods who had helped shape Divine Orr.

Yet physical evidence survived.

His hidden reliquary remained in Siren's Landing. Forgotten monuments and buried symbols preserved traces of his place among the Six.

Arah itself had been built during an age when he was still part of the pantheon.

The religious memory of the post-Exodus world could therefore be changed more easily than the stones beneath it.

XII

The age after the Gods

The Exodus ended the age of direct Divine rule, but it did not begin an age without religion, magic, or the influence of the Gods.

It began something more complicated.

Humanity inherited temples built while the Gods were physically present, magical systems shaped by their decisions, kingdoms founded beneath their influence, and histories written by people who still remembered a world in which Divine beings could be encountered face to face.

The Gods remained beyond the world.

Their followers remained within it.

From this point forward, the history of Humanity would increasingly become the history of what mortals chose to do with the world the Gods had left behind.

Archive Note

What the Exodus does not tell us

The Exodus is one of the defining events of Human history, yet the surviving record preserves its consequences more clearly than the private decisions that caused it.

The individual decisions of the Five

The surviving histories establish a collective withdrawal. They do not preserve a complete account of what each God argued, feared, regretted, or hoped would follow.

The manner of departure

The Gods departed Tyria for the Mists, but the surviving record does not provide a detailed account of the physical ceremony or exact process by which the Five left Arah.

What remained in Arah

Arah retained extensive Divine and ancient collections after the Exodus, but there is no complete surviving inventory of what the Gods took with them and what they intentionally left behind.

The limits of non-intervention

The Gods ceased living physically in Tyria, but Divine influence continued through worship, realms, servants, blessings, visions, and other connections with the Mists. The Exodus should therefore not be described as complete silence.

The safest distinction is also the most important: the Gods stopped ruling Tyria through physical presence. They did not cease to exist, and mortals did not cease reaching toward them.

End of Volume V

The world belongs to those who remain

The Gods had arrived from the Mists, lived among their followers, gathered the relics of older civilizations, governed from Arah, expanded and restricted mortal magic, fought one of their own, and finally withdrawn from the physical world.

Their departure did not bring peace.

It removed one of the powers that had previously stood above mortal kingdoms.

Human civilizations would now contend increasingly with one another, with neighboring peoples, and with the consequences of the Divine Age without the Gods physically present to settle those conflicts.
